Attention in Action: How Models Select and Combine Context

Calculate how attention combines context, follow how it learns, and see what masks, caches and faster algorithms each change.

Prerequisite: chapters 01-06, refreshed at point of use15 sectionsOriginal diagrams + explained numerical experiments

The engineering question

You inspect an attention implementation. It produces the expected tensor shape, but does it mix the right positions, use the right denominator, and reuse only valid cached states? A plausible-looking matrix is not enough. We will make the computation small enough to audit by hand.

Chapter 6 followed data through a complete decoder. Here we look more closely at attention. First we calculate its outputs and the gradients used to learn its weights. Then we compare masks, caches and ways to reduce work or storage. Section 13 puts the calculation into C#. The Interview questions section has ten prompts with explained answers.

Small examples, clear assumptions

We choose small numbers so you can check each step. They are teaching examples, not measurements from a trained model. Unless stated otherwise, we leave out biases (added offsets) and dropout (randomly omitting and rescaling some quantities during training).

Equations describe exact real-number arithmetic. Reported decimals and computer calculations are approximations. The examples show what a head calculates, not a guaranteed grammatical job or a measured speedup.

1. Separate matching information from contributed information

Suppose a code assistant sees "The worker released the lease after it expired." Useful context may relate "it" to the lease rather than the worker. This motivates contextual representations: intermediate numbers that depend on other permitted positions. It does not prove that an attention head resolves this sentence correctly.

Think of a code-search tool. A search request says what to look for. Each candidate has information used to match it, then content to return. Attention separates three roles in a similar way:

  • A query is a vector used to ask, "Which positions should this position draw from?"
  • A key is a vector the query compares against to get a matching score.
  • A value is the vector that contributes to the result once the matching weights are known.

The comparison helps explain the roles, but attention works with learned numbers. Its queries are not text searches, and its values are not stored documents or verified facts.

Recall chapter 1's shape rules. A matrix is a table; its shape gives the number of rows and columns. Our state table X has N rows, one per token position, and d columns, one per feature. Its entries are invented numerical features, not token IDs or physical measurements.

Token tables store vectors as rows. That is why we write XW, rather than the Wx used for individual column vectors in chapter 1. The weight layouts are transposes of each other. For now we follow one sequence and one head; section 6 adds batches and heads.

Q=XWQ,K=XWK,V=XWVQ=XW_Q,\qquad K=XW_K,\qquad V=XW_VQ=XWQ​,K=XWK​,V=XWV​

WQ and WK have shape d-by-dk; WV has shape d-by-dv. Here dk is the query/key width and dv is the value width, both positive integers. Each multiplication keeps N rows. It produces Q and K with dk columns, and V with dv columns.

Queries and keys need equal widths so their coordinates can be multiplied in pairs. Values can have a different width because they take a different path. WQ, WK and WV are learned parameters, stored between requests. Q, K and V are activations: numbers calculated from the current input. In a pre-normalized block, X is the normalized input to attention, not necessarily the original embedding table lookup.

Matrix multiplication combines a row with each output column. Multiply corresponding entries and add: row [a,b] times column [c,e] produces ac+be. A transpose, written T as a superscript, swaps rows and columns; it is not an inverse. A dot product is this coordinate-by-coordinate multiply-and-sum comparison. Distinct query/key maps mean their score table need not be symmetric or have a constant diagonal.

Follow three positions through the calculation

Choose N=3 and d=dk=dv=2: three positions, with two coordinates in every vector. The labels "worker", "lease" and "expired" help us follow the rows. They do not describe real tokenizer output or assign meanings to the coordinates.

We need two reminders before choosing the numbers. A square root is the nonnegative number that gives the original number when squared. The square root of 2 is about 1.414214; we write sqrt2 in prose.

The exponential exp(z) means e raised to z, where e is about 2.71828. The natural logarithm reverses it: ln(2) is the input that makes exp return 2. Thus exp(ln2)=2, exp(ln3)=3 and exp(0)=1. Negative exponents give reciprocals: exp(-ln3)=1/exp(ln3)=1/3. Logarithms need positive inputs. Chapter 2 explains these functions in more detail.

These outputs are weighted averages, also called convex mixtures: every weight is nonnegative, and the weights add to one. An average cannot exceed its largest input or fall below its smallest input. Our first output coordinates therefore stay between 0 and 2, and our second coordinates between 0 and 4.

This check applies to the attention mixture itself. A later output projection or residual addition can move outside those ranges. Also, attention is not one fixed linear average: changing the input can change the matching weights as well as the values.

3. Scaling controls a distribution, not a maximum allowed score

Why divide by square root of key width rather than key width itself? The motivation concerns statistical spread under assumptions, not a bound on trained scores. Refresh chapter 6's variance lesson: a mean is sum divided by count; a deviation is an outcome minus the mean; variance is the mean squared deviation; standard deviation is its nonnegative square root.

Independent means choosing one sign does not change the other sign's probabilities. Each sign is equally likely to be -1 or 1, with mean (-1+1)/2=0 and variance (1+1)/2=1. Their four equally likely sums have mean (-2+0+0+2)/4=0 and variance (4+0+0+4)/4=2. Divide each sum by sqrt2: every squared deviation is divided by 2, so variance becomes 2/2=1. Divide instead by 2: variance becomes 2/4=1/2.

Now connect the signs to a dot product. If a query coordinate and a key coordinate independently take equally likely values -1 or 1, their four possible products are [1,-1,-1,1]. The mean is (1-1-1+1)/4=0. The variance is (1+1+1+1)/4=1. A dot product adds products like these, so adding more independent coordinates increases its spread.

The usual scaling argument assumes query/key coordinates have mean zero, variance one, and are approximately independent both across coordinates and from each other. Under those assumptions, dk products have total variance about dk. Their standard deviation is about sqrt(dk). Dividing the score by sqrt(dk) brings that spread back near one.

Trained coordinates can be correlated or have different scales. The argument explains the choice of divisor; it does not guarantee a maximum score or equal attention weights.

Subtracting a maximum preserves the answer

Large exponentials can overflow a finite computer number. Numerical stability means evaluating an equivalent expression without unnecessarily producing problematic intermediate values. Subtract a row's maximum m from every permitted score. Since exp(s-m)=exp(s)/exp(m), all numerator and denominator terms share the same divisor and the weights stay unchanged.

Worked example / Stable arithmetic with a large common offset

For scores [1000,1000+ln3], do not calculate exp(1000). Subtract m=1000+ln3, giving [-ln3,0]. Exponentials are [1/3,1], sum 4/3. Weights are [(1/3)/(4/3),1/(4/3)]=[1/4,3/4]. With values 2 and 4, output is 0.5+3=3.5. This is exactly the mathematical distribution of [0,ln3] as well.

A very small positive computer result can round to zero; this is called underflow. It can happen to an exponential after subtracting the maximum. Decide what numerical error your application allows, but do not confuse that rounded zero with a mask that deliberately blocks a key. Inputs such as infinity or not-a-number need explicit handling; our code rejects them.

4. Follow a small loss backward through the mixture

How does training change attention? Start at the output loss and work backward. Ask how a small change in a value or a matching score would change that loss. Those sensitivities eventually tell us how to update the projection weights.

A derivative measures output change per small input change near a particular input. For f(x)=x squared, changing x from 2 to 2.001 changes f from 4 to 4.004001. Divide the changes: 0.004001/0.001=4.001. As the input step shrinks, this ratio approaches 4, the derivative at x=2. It describes nearby changes, not an exact prediction for any large step. Chapter 2 develops this limit.

Refresh chapter 2's chain rule: if changing x changes y, and changing y changes a loss, multiply the two local sensitivities to find that path's loss sensitivity. When several paths meet, add their contributions. A partial derivative measures sensitivity to one coordinate while holding the others fixed; a gradient collects those coordinate sensitivities. All the functions differentiated here are smooth for finite scores and a nonempty permitted key set.

We will use one query, two permitted keys, scalar values and dk=1, so the scale divisor is one. Let q=1, k1=0, k2=ln3, v1=2 and v2=4. Scores are [0,ln3], weights [1/4,3/4], output o=(1/4)(2)+(3/4)(4)=3.5. Choose target y=3 and squared-error loss L=(o-y) squared. This is a local teaching objective, not a language model's actual next-token loss.

Why one score affects the other weight

Let D=exp(s1)+exp(s2). The weights are a1=exp(s1)/D and a2=exp(s2)/D. Here D names a positive denominator, not the model width. If s1 rises, a1's numerator rises, but the shared denominator rises too. We must account for both changes.

The derivative of exp(s) is exp(s). To differentiate a fraction n/D, use the quotient rule: (n'D - nD') / D squared. A prime here means a derivative, and D squared means D times D. The first term accounts for the numerator changing. The subtracted term accounts for the denominator changing.

Why subtract? A larger numerator raises a fraction; a larger positive denominator lowers it. If dn and dD are tiny changes, the change in n/D is approximately dn/D minus n dD/D squared. Divide by the input step and let that step shrink to get the quotient rule. Apply it separately to the two weights:

Worked backward pass / Both paths contribute

At scores [0,ln3], exponentials are [1,3] and D=4. The first quotient derivative has numerator 1(4) - 1(1) = 3 and denominator 4(4) = 16, giving 3/16. The cross derivative is -(3)(1)/16 = -3/16. Equivalently at weights [1/4,3/4], own-score sensitivities are (1/4)(3/4) = 3/16 = 0.1875 for a1 and (3/4)(1/4) = 3/16 for a2. Increasing s1 slightly raises a1 and lowers a2 by equal first-order amounts.

With o=a1v1+a2v2, output sensitivity to s1 adds v1(3/16) and v2(-3/16). Substitute the values: 2(3/16) - 4(3/16) = 6/16 - 12/16 = -6/16 = -0.375. For s2 it is 2(-3/16) + 4(3/16) = -6/16 + 12/16 = 0.375. Raising the score for the smaller value lowers the output.

Holding scores fixed, output sensitivity to v1 is a1=1/4 and to v2 is a2=3/4. L=(3.5-3) squared=0.25. Its derivative with respect to o is 2(o-y)=2(0.5)=1. Multiplying by this incoming sensitivity leaves score gradients [-0.375,0.375] and value gradients [0.25,0.75].

For any number of permitted scalar values, the same branch-sum reasoning simplifies to:

∂o∂sj=aj(vj−o)\frac{\partial o}{\partial s_j}=a_j(v_j-o)∂sj​∂o​=aj​(vj​−o)

j identifies one permitted key. vj is that key's scalar value; aj is its weight; o is the whole weighted output. This derivative describes how o changes when score sj changes.

To see where the short rule comes from, separate key j's path from all the others. Its own path contributes vj aj(1-aj), which is aj vj minus aj squared times vj. Every other path subtracts aj times that path's weighted value. Together, all the subtracted terms are aj times the complete output o. The result is aj(vj-o).

The rule also gives an intuition: raising a score pulls the output toward that key's value. A value above the current output gives a positive derivative; a value below it gives a negative derivative. For vector values, do this one output coordinate at a time, then combine with the incoming loss sensitivities.

Worked backward pass / Queries, keys and a stored coefficient

Since sj=qkj at width one, its derivative with respect to q is kj; with respect to kj it is q. Thus dL/dq=(-0.375)(0)+(0.375)(ln3)=0.375ln3, approximately 0.411980. Each key receives dL/dkj=(dL/dsj)q, giving [-0.375,0.375]. Values still receive [0.25,0.75]; they are not used in the query-key dot products.

Suppose this query came from one projection q=xwQ with fixed x=2 and stored wQ=0.5. Then dq/dwQ=x=2, so dL/dwQ=2(0.375ln3)=0.75ln3, approximately 0.823959. Saturation means the distribution is very concentrated. With a weight 0.999, its own-score derivative is 0.999(1-0.999)=0.000999; at 0.5 it is 0.5(0.5)=0.25. Small local weight derivatives can make some matching gradients small. Actual loss gradients also depend on values and incoming sensitivities, so concentration does not by itself prove all gradients vanish. Scaling helps manage score spread, not guarantee optimization.

5. Define access before normalizing

A causal mask permits a query at absolute position i to use key positions j at most i, including itself. That row predicts the following token, not the one it has already received. Refresh chapter 6's shifted targets: inputs [worker,lease,expired] can have targets [lease,expired,<end>]. A known training sequence allows parallel computation, but future input rows must not leak each row's target.

A one-row decode query can see a long prefix

Suppose a cache contains absolute positions 1, 2, 3 and a new token occupies position 4. The decode Q tensor contains one row, but its position is 4. It must compare with keys 1 through 4, including the newly appended key. A naive mask j at most local row 1 would incorrectly permit only key 1.

Worked example / Locate the query before masking

Let P=3 be the cached prefix length and t=1 the new query's local position, counted from one. Its absolute position is i=P+t=4. The causal rule j at most P+t permits j=1,2,3,4. Serving/training libraries may handle padded query rows separately; that behavior must be intentional.

6. Track positions and coordinates through multiple heads

Recall the steps from chapter 6: each head calculates its own queries, keys, values and weighted result. We then concatenate the results, meaning place their coordinates end to end. A learned output matrix mixes those joined coordinates.

A head's projection can read every input coordinate. We split its output coordinates into head groups. We do not give each head an isolated slice of the original embedding or assign it one type of meaning.

A tensor is an array with labeled axes. Each axis tells us what an index selects: an example, a position, a head or a coordinate. Let B count examples in a batch and Hq count query heads. Instead of making a separate matrix call for every head, a fused projection calculates all their query coordinates at once.

Here X has shape [B,N,d], and WQ is d-by-(Hq dk). Multiplication produces [B,N,Hq dk]. First reshape the last axis into [Hq,dk]: this labels the head groups without changing the numbers. Then swap the position and head axes to put all positions for each head together. The table follows that rearrangement.

The comparison rule is unchanged, but its score table can be rectangular. An encoder-decoder commonly lets target queries read the entire already-known nonpadding source, while target self-attention remains causal.

9. Prefill and decode use different query counts

Generation has two stages. Prefill processes the known prompt. Each layer calculates keys and values for its prompt positions. The final relevant prompt state produces logits, the vocabulary scores used to choose the first new token.

Decode then processes that chosen token to predict the next one, usually one token per step per sequence. For example, a three-token prompt predicts token 4; processing token 4 predicts token 5. The language-model head in chapter 6 turns the final state into vocabulary scores. Attention alone does not choose a token.

A KV cache saves the keys and values already calculated at each layer. It does not store projection weights, response text or external documents. Why can we reuse it? With causal attention, adding token 4 cannot change what tokens 1-3 were allowed to read. If computation is deterministic and the inputs and model stay the same, their old keys and values stay valid.

The unchanged-input condition matters. Editing an earlier token, changing the model weights, or changing the masks or position rules can make the old cache invalid.

Worked example / Cached and full causal arithmetic agree

Return to section 1's projections, used here as one causal attention layer with fixed incoming rows. Prefill positions 1 and 2 stores K=[[ln2,0],[0,ln3]] and V=[[2,0],[0,4]]. Position 2's causal output is [1/2,3]. A real downstream language head would use the final prompt state to select a new token; stipulate that its next incoming branch row is [1,1], matching our position 3 fixture.

Compute only that new row: Q3=[sqrt2,sqrt2], K3=[ln2,ln3], V3=[2,4]. Append K3/V3 to the two cached rows. Q3 compares with all three keys; scaled scores are [ln2,ln3,ln6], weights [2/11,3/11,6/11], output [16/11,36/11]. This equals row 3 of the full causal pass. The old queries are unnecessary for this new output.

In a multi-layer model, layer 1 first calculates the new token's output. Layer 2 uses that output as its new input and has its own cache. Continue the same way through the layers: do not copy layer 1's cache into layer 2.

This reuse depends on causal attention. In bidirectional self-attention, an old position may read a newly added token. Its state can then change, so the same reuse argument does not apply.

A matrix-matrix product can project several known token rows together. A single decode row's projection is a row-vector-by-matrix product. These operation shapes explain why one-request decode can expose different bottlenecks from a large prefill. They do not dictate every GPU kernel: batching independent requests, processing chunks or using another decoding algorithm can create multiple rows.

10. Sharing keys reduces cache storage, not query heads

The question here is simple: how many query heads reuse the same keys and values? Let Hq count query heads and Hkv count key/value head pairs. One KV pair includes both a key head and a value head; we do not count them twice.

  • MHA, conventional multi-head attention: each query head has its own KV pair, so Hkv=Hq.
  • GQA, grouped-query attention: a group of query heads shares one KV pair, so there are fewer KV pairs than query heads.
  • MQA, multi-query attention: every query head shares one KV pair, so Hkv=1.

The model must be trained or adapted to use those projections and group assignments. Allocating a smaller cache does not by itself convert an MHA model into GQA.

11. Count comparisons separately from allocated memory

If a prompt doubles in length, does attention do twice the work or four times the work? Computational complexity describes that growth. It is not a time measurement.

Big-O notation describes an upper growth rate, leaving out constant factors and smaller terms. Here full attention compares N queries with N keys, giving N squared pairs per head. We call that quadratic growth: doubling N gives four times as many pairs. Actual runtime also depends on parallel execution, data movement, the implementation and the hardware.

For Nq query positions and Nk key positions per example/head, the dense score table has B Hq Nq Nk entries. Each query-key dot product uses dk multiplications and dk-1 additions. Mixing dv value coordinates uses dv multiplications per pair, and sums across keys for each output coordinate. These counts isolate the two matrix products; projections, scaling, masks, exponentials and backward work are additional.

Worked example / Full prefill at a small fixed width

Choose B=1, Hq=2, Nq=Nk=N=4, dk=dv=2. Pairs are 1(2)(4)(4)=32. Score multiplications: 32(2)=64; score additions: 32(2-1)=32. Weighted-value multiplications: 32(2)=64. There are 1(2)(4)(2)=16 output coordinates, each summing four terms using three additions: 16(3)=48. Thus the two products together use 128 multiplications and 80 additions.

If N doubles to 8, pairs become 1(2)(8)(8)=128, four times 32. Score and value multiplications total 128(2)+128(2)=512, four times 128. Addition counts are 128(1)+[1(2)(8)(2)](7)=128+224=352; these are not exactly four times 80 because sums use Nk-1 additions. Growth-rate shorthand is not exact operation accounting.

12. Tiling can preserve full attention without storing full weights

So far we have pictured a complete table of scores and a complete table of weights. Do we need to keep both tables in memory to calculate the output? No. We can process small blocks and keep running sums.

FlashAttention uses this idea to reduce data movement between memory levels, often called IO. A GPU has large high-bandwidth memory and smaller, faster on-chip storage. A tile is a small block of queries, keys and values processed together. The algorithm combines each tile's contribution without storing every score and weight in large memory.

It still calculates attention over the permitted keys. The mathematical result is the same in exact arithmetic; computer results can differ slightly because the order of floating-point operations changes.

Chapter 6 used two scalar values to introduce stable accumulation. Now we will merge a two-key tile with a third key and preserve both value coordinates. This is the algebra behind online softmax, not a GPU kernel, its full scheduling or its backward implementation.

Keep enough information to merge a tile

After processing at least one permitted key, keep three running quantities:

  • m: the largest score seen so far. We subtract it before taking exponentials, as in stable softmax.
  • l: the sum of those shifted exponentials. This is a lowercase letter L, not the number one.
  • z: the sum of values multiplied by those same shifted exponentials. This is a vector with dv coordinates.

Divide z by l when you want the weighted output. Both sums use the same subtracted maximum, so its effect cancels:

Worked example / First tile, keys 1 and 2

Use query 3 from section 2: scores [ln2,ln3,ln6], values [2,0],[0,4],[2,4]. The first tile's maximum is m=ln3. Its shifted exponentials are [exp(ln2-ln3),exp(0)]=[2/3,1]. Thus l=2/3+1=5/3.

Coordinate 1 of z is (2/3)(2)+1(0)=4/3. Coordinate 2 is (2/3)(0)+1(4)=4. Before the third key, the partial normalized output would be [(4/3)/(5/3),4/(5/3)]=[4/5,12/5]. This is attention over two keys only, not the finished output.

Merge under a common maximum

The second tile has one score t=ln6 and value v=[2,4]. Its score is larger than the old maximum ln3. Update the maximum to m'=max(m,t)=ln6. A prime here means "updated," not a derivative.

We cannot directly add sums that used different subtracted maxima. Convert the old sums to the new reference first. Multiply both by alpha=exp(m-m')=exp(ln3-ln6)=1/2. The new key has shifted exponential u=exp(t-m')=exp(0)=1. Now add its contribution:

Worked example / Finish both coordinates

l'=(1/2)(5/3)+1=5/6+6/6=11/6. First numerator coordinate: (1/2)(4/3)+1(2)=2/3+2=8/3. Second: (1/2)(4)+1(4)=2+4=6. Divide: o1=(8/3)/(11/6)=(8/3)(6/11)=16/11; o2=6/(11/6)=6(6/11)=36/11. This is exactly the full-row answer, without storing its three final weights.

A tile can contain more than one key. First calculate that tile's maximum mt, exponential sum lt and weighted-value sum zt. To merge it with the running values:

  1. Choose the larger maximum: m'=max(m,mt).
  2. Multiply both old sums, l and z, by exp(m-m').
  3. Multiply both tile sums, lt and zt, by exp(mt-m').
  4. Add the two denominator sums and the two numerator vectors.

Both parts now use the same maximum, so we can combine them. Start from the first tile with a permitted key; skip fully blocked tiles. Tile order does not change the exact result. With floating-point numbers, regrouping additions can change the last few digits: (a+b)+c need not equal a+(b+c) exactly on a computer.

Full attention still evaluates its permitted query-key comparisons: tiling does not remove quadratic full-prefill arithmetic. Savings target intermediate storage, memory traffic and kernel efficiency. Cache storage is another cost; sharing KV heads addresses it differently. No universal speedup follows from this miniature calculation.

13. Calculate one query's output in C#

The following CPU example calculates one query's output. Give it an already-projected query, keys and values. A Boolean mask says which keys it may use, and a positive divisor sets the score scale. The numbers use double, C#'s double-precision floating-point type, and must be finite.

For standard scaled attention, pass sqrt(query width) as the divisor. Pass 1 only for our unscaled comparison. The function checks dimensions and reports invalid inputs rather than returning a plausible but incorrect result. It is a small reference calculation, not a trained Transformer or an optimized GPU routine.

Predict the three causal outputs before running it. Copy the complete block into Program.cs of a local .NET 9 console project; no ML package is required. Inputs must be nonnull arrays with nonnull rows. Math.Exp computes the exponential, Math.Sqrt computes a square root, and bool[] explicitly describes allowed keys. Add one to a zero-based array index to obtain the chapter's one-based position: code index 0 is position 1.

using System;

static class AttentionReference
{
    static void RequireFinite(double[] row)
    {
        foreach (double value in row)
            if (!double.IsFinite(value))
                throw new ArgumentException("Entries must be finite.");
    }

    public static double[] Compute(
        double[] query, double[][] keys, double[][] values,
        bool[] allowed, double divisor)
    {
        if (query.Length == 0 || keys.Length == 0 ||
            keys.Length != values.Length ||
            keys.Length != allowed.Length ||
            !double.IsFinite(divisor) || divisor <= 0)
            throw new ArgumentException("Invalid row contract.");
        RequireFinite(query);
        int width = values[0].Length;
        if (width == 0)
            throw new ArgumentException("Value width must be positive.");
        var scores = new double[keys.Length];
        double maximum = double.NegativeInfinity;
        for (int j = 0; j < keys.Length; j++)
        {
            if (keys[j].Length != query.Length ||
                values[j].Length != width)
                throw new ArgumentException("Inconsistent widths.");
            RequireFinite(keys[j]);
            RequireFinite(values[j]);
            if (!allowed[j]) continue;
            double dot = 0;
            for (int r = 0; r < query.Length; r++)
                dot += query[r] * keys[j][r];
            scores[j] = dot / divisor;
            if (!double.IsFinite(scores[j]))
                throw new ArithmeticException("Score overflow.");
            maximum = Math.Max(maximum, scores[j]);
        }
        if (!double.IsFinite(maximum))
            throw new ArgumentException("No permitted key.");
        var weights = new double[keys.Length];
        double total = 0;
        for (int j = 0; j < keys.Length; j++)
        {
            if (!allowed[j]) continue;
            weights[j] = Math.Exp(scores[j] - maximum);
            total += weights[j];
        }
        var output = new double[width];
        for (int j = 0; j < keys.Length; j++)
        {
            if (!allowed[j]) continue;
            double weight = weights[j] / total;
            for (int c = 0; c < width; c++)
                output[c] += weight * values[j][c];
        }
        RequireFinite(output);
        return output;
    }

    public static void Main()
    {
        double root = Math.Sqrt(2);
        double[][] q = { new[] { root, 0.0 },
            new[] { 0.0, root }, new[] { root, root } };
        double[][] k = { new[] { Math.Log(2), 0.0 },
            new[] { 0.0, Math.Log(3) },
            new[] { Math.Log(2), Math.Log(3) } };
        double[][] v = { new[] { 2.0, 0.0 },
            new[] { 0.0, 4.0 }, new[] { 2.0, 4.0 } };
        for (int i = 0; i < q.Length; i++)
        {
            var mask = new bool[k.Length];
            for (int j = 0; j < mask.Length; j++)
                mask[j] = j <= i;
            double[] o = Compute(q[i], k, v, mask, root);
            Console.WriteLine(
                $"{i + 1}: [{o[0]:F6}, {o[1]:F6}]");
        }
    }
}

14. Interview questions

Explain what each mechanism does before naming its abbreviation. Use the worked numbers to support your answer. These ten questions apply the chapter's ideas to engineering decisions.

01 / When does attention connect two sequences rather than contextualize one?

Explain the answer

Self-attention calculates Q/K/V from one sequence. Cross-attention takes queries from a target sequence and K/V from a separate source sequence. Two target positions comparing with three source positions give a 2-by-3 score table. There is still one output row per target, so two output rows.

In our example, the first target divides exponentials [2,1,2] by their sum 5. The resulting weights mix values [2,0],[0,4],[2,4] to produce [8/5,12/5]. Target position 1 may read source position 3 if that source is already available. Source padding and the target's causal self-attention mask are separate rules. Simply adding retrieved text to a decoder prompt does not add a cross-attention module. Review the rectangular example.

02 / What parallelism does attention enable, and what remains dependent?

Explain the answer

Known positions can be projected and compared together within a layer, and permitted distant positions have direct attention paths. A recurrence instead uses its preceding state: for h_t=x_t+0.5h_(t-1), h0=0 and inputs [2,0,1], h1=2, h2=1, h3=1.5, in that order. Attention's three rows use a single 3-by-3 score operation instead of that recurrent state chain. However layer 2 still needs layer 1, and ordinary token 5 generation needs the selected token 4. Caching avoids repeated valid old K/V, not unknown-token dependencies. A quadratic full-prefill pair count is a tradeoff, not proof that one architecture always wins. Refresh the recurrence and review the generation timeline.

03 / Why distinguish a key from a value if both come from the same state?

Explain the answer

Keys help decide how much to take; values supply what to combine. Separate learned maps let these roles use different coordinates. With q=1 and keys [0,ln3], softmax gives weights [1/4,3/4]. Values [2,4] give output 3.5. Change only the second value to 8: the output becomes (1/4)(2) + (3/4)(8) = 0.5 + 6 = 6.5. The matching weights do not change.

Q/K/V are calculated activations; WQ/WK/WV are stored parameters. The search-metadata/content analogy helps separate their roles, but attention is not literally a document index, and its coordinates have no assigned semantic labels. Review projection arithmetic.

04 / Why scale by square root of key width, and why not call it a guarantee?

Explain the answer

Under approximately independent, centered unit-variance coordinate assumptions, adding dk query-key products yields variance about dk. A divisor sqrt(dk) therefore restores variance near one. At dk=4, divide raw scores [2,0] by 2 to get [1,0]. The larger weight changes from about 0.880797 to 0.731059. A saturated weight 0.999 has own-score sensitivity 0.000999, versus 0.25 at weight 0.5; scaling can help avoid excessively concentrated distributions. Trained correlations can violate the assumptions, and value/loss sensitivities also matter. The divisor concerns query/key width, not necessarily model width, and is not a causal mask. Review the variance table and the backward paths.

05 / How do MHA, GQA and MQA differ without deleting query heads?

Explain the answer

They differ in how queries share keys and values. MHA gives each query head its own KV pair. GQA shares a KV pair within each query group. MQA shares one KV pair across all query heads. In all three cases, every query head still calculates an output.

With four query heads, our MHA/GQA/MQA examples have four, two and one KV pairs. Under B=1,L=2,S=6,dk=2,dv=3,b=2, their payloads are 480, 240 and 120 bytes. Sharing does not force equal outputs: queries [1] and [-1] using the same keys [0,ln3] return 3.5 and 2.5.

Cache size depends on Hkv; the number of query outputs depends on Hq. The model needs compatible learned projections and group assignments. A smaller allocation alone does not create a GQA model or guarantee better speed or quality. Review shapes and group ownership.

06 / What exactly must a next-token causal mask prohibit?

Explain the answer

Query position i may use keys j at most i, including its own input, while predicting token i+1. It cannot use later input rows that expose the answer. Our row 2 retains exponentials [1,3], denominator 4, output [0.5,3]. Dropping the third term from weights normalized by 7 gives the wrong denominator. For one decode query after P=3 cached positions, local t=1 corresponds to absolute i=4, so keys 1-4 are allowed. Padding can impose additional exclusions, and an all-blocked row needs explicit handling. Position encodings do not enforce this access rule. Review the mask and offset examples.

07 / What does the KV cache save, and when is reuse valid?

Explain the answer

It saves layer-specific computed keys/values for a compatible causal prefix. Old states do not depend on newly appended future tokens, so the new query can reuse them. Appending section 9's K3/V3 gives scores [ln2,ln3,ln6], weights [2/11,3/11,6/11] and output [16/11,36/11], equal to full causal row 3. Each layer has its own cache; old query rows and final response text are not the cached payload. Editing the prefix, changing weights or position conventions can invalidate reuse. C=BLSHkv(dk+dv)b counts raw bytes, not every allocation or model tensor. Review reuse and the units.

08 / How can FlashAttention avoid full intermediate matrices and still be exact?

Explain the answer

It processes blocks and keeps running sums instead of keeping every weight. After scores [ln2,ln3], our first tile has maximum m=ln3, exponential sum l=5/3 and weighted-value sum z=[4/3,4]. The next score ln6 raises the maximum. Rescale both old sums by 1/2, then add the new shifted exponential 1 and its weighted value [2,4]. This gives l'=11/6 and z'=[8/3,6]. Dividing z' by l' gives [16/11,36/11], the full-row result.

The essential step is rescaling both sums together. We save storage for full intermediate tables; we do not remove permitted query-key comparisons. The algebra is exact. Different floating-point operation orders may change the last few digits, but this is not permission for an arbitrary approximation or a claim of one universal speedup. Review both tile orders.

09 / When is attention quadratic, and when is a step linear in context?

Explain the answer

During full prefill, N queries compare with N keys per head: N squared pairs. At B=1,Hq=2,N=4,dk=dv=2, the score and value products use 128 multiplications. Double N to 8 and they use 512, four times as many. Causal attention permits N(N+1)/2 pairs, which is still quadratic. Also, masking a dense table does not necessarily reduce the memory allocated for it.

One cached decode step has only one new query per head. It compares with S keys, so doubling S doubles that step's work. With our same widths, S=4 uses 32 multiplications; S=8 uses 64. This is linear growth in S. A whole generated sequence adds many such steps, with a growing cache at each step.

Keep three questions separate: how many operations, how many stored bytes, and how much measured time? Big-O addresses growth, not milliseconds. KV storage is also different from temporary score/weight storage. Review exact counts and assumptions.

10 / Does a high attention weight establish why an answer was produced?

Explain the answer

No. It describes one head/query's mixture, not a complete causal explanation of the final output. For weights [0.9,0.1] and scalar values [0,100], contributions are 0.9(0)=0 and 0.1(100)=10: the smaller weight supplies the entire scalar output. Vector directions can also cancel, and output projection, residual paths, FFNs and later layers change how mixed states affect logits. Heads may have overlapping or hard-to-interpret patterns. Attention also cannot retrieve an unseen external paragraph or authorize an action; RAG and permission checks are separate system operations. Review the scope of an attention inspection.

15. Check what attention can and cannot tell you

A useful inspection, not a complete explanation

Attention weights answer a narrow question: how did one head distribute its permitted value mixture for this query? They do not establish factual relevance, trust, grammatical specialization or a causal explanation of the generated answer. The counterexample weights [0.9,0.1] and values [0,100] produce output 10 entirely through the smaller-weight term. If values were [10,-90], the same weights would give 9-9=0: vector/scalar contributions can cancel.

RAG, retrieval-augmented generation, is an application pipeline that obtains external information and supplies selected text to a model. Attention ordinarily mixes representations already in the model's current permitted context. Retrieved text can enter a decoder prefix and then receive attention; high weight does not authenticate that text or authorize a tool call. The retrieval lesson in chapter 4 and application boundary in chapter 6 remain relevant.

Five ideas to keep

  • Projection: queries and keys decide matching weights; values supply the numbers to combine. Check what each axis means before multiplying.
  • Normalization: each query divides by the sum for its own permitted keys. Scaling, masking and subtracting a maximum solve different problems.
  • Learning: both the matching and value paths carry gradients. Add contributions from every use of a shared parameter before updating it.
  • Reuse: save each layer's valid keys and values. Use the new token's actual position, not just its row number in a small array.
  • Efficiency: windows change which keys can be read; GQA changes which heads share K/V; tiling changes how calculations are stored and run. A byte saving is not a measured speedup.

Synthesis / Audit a claimed optimization

An engineer says: "Four-query-head GQA with two KV heads makes full attention linear, and a one-row decode query can only read key 1. FlashAttention then deletes distant keys." Correct each claim. For B=1,L=2,S=6,dk=dv=2,b=2, calculate GQA cache bytes.

Reveal solution

Two KV heads reduce stored K/V, not the four query heads' full-prefix comparisons. Full prefill still has N squared comparisons per query head. A decode row's absolute position accounts for its cached prefix: after five old positions, its local row 1 corresponds to position 6 and may read keys 1-6. FlashAttention evaluates the specified permitted set with tiled stable accumulation; a sliding window, not exact full-attention tiling, excludes distant keys.

Payload is 1(2)(6)(2)(2+2)(2). Compute 1(2)=2, then 2(6)=12, 12(2)=24, 24(4)=96 scalars, and 96(2)=192 bytes. Model weights, intermediates, allocation overhead and data-type metadata are excluded. A correct payload number alone cannot certify a faster or more accurate serving implementation.
