The Language of LLMs: Vectors, Matrices, and Meaning

A language for representing information, transforming it, and deciding what to combine.

Start here: no linear algebra assumed9 sectionsWorked examples + an interactive lab

The question we are answering

A language model receives token IDs and produces scores for the next token. What mathematical objects carry the information between those two points?

Imagine building a search feature for a support center. Someone types, "I can't access my account." A useful result might say "Reset your sign-in credentials," even though the words differ. Exact string matching is not enough. We need a numerical representation that makes useful comparisons possible.

Linear algebra supplies the containers and operations: vectors represent, matrices transform, and weighted sums combine. Training determines which numerical representations and transformations are useful. The arithmetic alone does not give a model an understanding of language.

By the end, you will be able to trace tensor shapes through one attention head, distinguish similarity from dependence, and explain why a small low-rank update can adapt a large weight matrix.

1. A vector is an ordered representation

First, learn to read the notation

You need arithmetic, not a previous mathematics course. A scalar is one number: 3 requests, a weight of -0.5, or a price of 2. A letter is a placeholder for a value. In 2x+12x+12x+1, substitute 3 for x, multiply first, then add: 2(3)+1=72(3)+1=72(3)+1=7. An equation states that two expressions have the same value; ≈\approx≈ means approximately equal.

Subscripts label entries, not multiplication: x2x_2x2​ means entry 2 of x. Superscripts sometimes mean powers: 32=3(3)=93^2=3(3)=932=3(3)=9. But a superscript ⊤\top⊤ means transpose, not a power; we will spell out that operation. We count entries from 1 in mathematics even if your code indexes from 0. Bold lowercase letters denote vectors; uppercase letters denote matrices.

Start with a delivery robot. Its displacement could be 3 meters east and 2 meters north. The order matters: swapping the coordinates describes a different movement. A vector is an ordered list of numbers that we can add and scale under consistent rules.

x=[32]∈R2,∥x∥2=32+22=13≈3.606\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad \|\mathbf{x}\|_2=\sqrt{3^2+2^2}=\sqrt{13}\approx3.606x=[32​]∈R2,∥x∥2​=32+22​=13​≈3.606

x\mathbf{x}x is the vector; R2\mathbb{R}^{2}R2 means two real-valued coordinates; ∥x∥2\|\mathbf{x}\|_2∥x∥2​ is its Euclidean length. A scalar is a single number, such as 2.

The symbol ∈\in∈ means "belongs to"; R\mathbb RR is the real number line, including negatives and fractions. A vector's dimension or width is its number of coordinates. We draw two coordinates as an arrow from the origin, the point [0,0]. A third coordinate adds another axis; 64 coordinates obey the same arithmetic without a literal 64-axis drawing.

Why the square root in the length formula? East and north form a right triangle. The Pythagorean rule says its squared diagonal length is 32+22=133^2+2^2=1332+22=13. The square root selects the nonnegative length whose square is 13. This length is also called the Euclidean norm; the subscript 2 identifies the sum-of-squares choice. A zero vector has all coordinates zero and length zero.

The robot gives the coordinates physical meanings. In other applications, we choose a consistent schema: a house could be represented by area, number of rooms, and age. Those coordinates have different units, so a raw Euclidean distance can be dominated by area. Feature scaling and the choice of distance metric (the distance rule) are part of the design, not afterthoughts.

For example, compare house features [100,2] and [110,3], using area and rooms. The difference is [10,1], with squared length 102+12=10110^2+1^2=101102+12=101: the area coordinate supplies 100 of that total. Dividing every area by 10 changes the difference to [1,1] and squared length to 2. This feature scaling changes the relative influence of coordinates; neither mixed-feature score is automatically a physical distance or a universally correct comparison.

Worked example / Combine movements

Two moves, one destination

The robot moves u=[3,2]⊤\mathbf{u}=[3,2]^\topu=[3,2]⊤, then v=[−1,2]⊤\mathbf{v}=[-1,2]^\topv=[−1,2]⊤. The superscript ⊤\top⊤ transposes a row into a column; we use column vectors for individual examples.

u+v=[24],2u=[64]\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\4\end{bmatrix},\qquad 2\mathbf{u}=\begin{bmatrix}6\\4\end{bmatrix}u+v=[24​],2u=[64​]

Add matching coordinates. Scaling by 2 doubles both coordinates; scaling by a negative number also reverses direction. In general, au+bva\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v}au+bv is a linear combination, where aaa and bbb are scalar coefficients.

Write out the first calculation: [3+(−1), 2+2]⊤=[2,4]⊤[3+(-1),\,2+2]^\top=[2,4]^\top[3+(−1),2+2]⊤=[2,4]⊤. Subtraction is addition of the negative: u−v=[3−(−1), 2−2]⊤=[4,0]⊤\mathbf u-\mathbf v=[3-(-1),\,2-2]^\top=[4,0]^\topu−v=[3−(−1),2−2]⊤=[4,0]⊤. Its length, 4, is the distance between the two arrow tips. You cannot add a two-coordinate displacement to a three-coordinate one: there is no matching third entry.

Coefficients need not be equal or positive. For example, 2u−v=[6,4]⊤−[−1,2]⊤=[7,2]⊤2\mathbf u-\mathbf v=[6,4]^\top-[-1,2]^\top=[7,2]^\top2u−v=[6,4]⊤−[−1,2]⊤=[7,2]⊤. A real vector space is a collection where these addition and scaling rules are consistently available and stay in the collection; all real two-coordinate vectors form one example. This mathematical closure does not make every sum a useful language representation.

From coordinates to embeddings

An embedding lookup maps a token ID to a learned vector. If a toy vocabulary contains 500 tokens and each embedding has 64 coordinates, its embedding table has shape 500×64500\times64500×64. The number of tokens and the number of coordinates are different quantities. "64-dimensional" means each vector has 64 entries; it does not mean the table has 64 vectors.

Actual learned coordinates generally do not come with labels like "royalty" or "happiness." Information can be distributed across many coordinates. Initial token embeddings are also different from the contextual hidden states produced as tokens pass through Transformer layers.

More coordinates provide more adjustable numerical degrees of freedom, not a fixed number of meanings. A single coordinate can already take infinitely many real values, so "64 dimensions can store exactly 64 concepts" is wrong. The appropriate width depends on the task, training, and computational budget. The word "bank" can also acquire different contextual states in a river sentence and a finance sentence even if it starts with the same token embedding.

Where the analogy stops

A 2D arrow is a visualization of arithmetic, not a map of language. Nearby learned vectors are only useful evidence of relatedness when the model, representation, and metric support that use. Arbitrarily adding two feature vectors does not guarantee a meaningful concept.

Inside a network, compatible vectors are often added: a residual connection adds a block's output to its input. Some architectures add positional embeddings to token embeddings. Others use methods such as rotary position embeddings (RoPE), which rotate query and key components rather than simply adding a position vector.

Worked example / Positions and offsets

For an additive-position toy model, token vector [0.2,−0.4]⊤[0.2,-0.4]^\top[0.2,−0.4]⊤ plus position vector [0.1,0.3]⊤[0.1,0.3]^\top[0.1,0.3]⊤ gives [0.3,−0.1]⊤[0.3,-0.1]^\top[0.3,−0.1]⊤. Both vectors must have width 2. A residual block returning [0.05,0.2]⊤[0.05,0.2]^\top[0.05,0.2]⊤ for the original token would instead produce [0.25,−0.2]⊤[0.25,-0.2]^\top[0.25,−0.2]⊤. The same arithmetic has different architectural roles.

An offset is a difference vector. In a deliberately fabricated representation, let "ticket" be [2,1], "tickets" [2,2], and "message" [5,1]. The plural offset is [2,2]−[2,1]=[0,1][2,2]-[2,1]=[0,1][2,2]−[2,1]=[0,1]; adding it to [5,1] predicts [5,2]. Real embedding analogies sometimes exhibit approximate offsets, but this construction is not evidence that real models encode grammar on coordinate 2, or that offset arithmetic reliably solves semantic tasks.

2. Dot products: contribution and alignment

A support search engine must rank candidate documents. A simple scoring operation is to multiply corresponding vector coordinates and add the contributions. That operation is a dot product.

a⊤b=∑i=1daibi=∥a∥2∥b∥2cos⁡θ\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{b}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_i b_i=\|\mathbf{a}\|_2\|\mathbf{b}\|_2\cos\thetaa⊤b=i=1∑d​ai​bi​=∥a∥2​∥b∥2​cosθ

ddd is the number of coordinates; iii indexes them; ai,bia_i,b_iai​,bi​ are the matching entries; θ\thetaθ is the angle between nonzero vectors.

The large symbol ∑\sum∑ means add a sequence of terms. For width 3, ∑i=13aibi=a1b1+a2b2+a3b3\sum_{i=1}^{3}a_i b_i=a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3∑i=13​ai​bi​=a1​b1​+a2​b2​+a3​b3​. Multiply corresponding entries before adding. The transpose makes a column into a row, so a row-by-column product produces one scalar, not another vector.

An angle measures how much one direction turns from another. For an acute angle in a right triangle, cosine is adjacent side divided by hypotenuse; the definition extends to angles between arbitrary nonzero vectors. Its important reference values are 1 for the same direction, 0 at a right angle (90 degrees), and -1 for opposite directions (180 degrees). That explains the sign of a dot product without attaching human meanings to the axes.

The coordinate formula and the geometric formula give the same number. The first explains how to compute it; the second explains why both direction and length matter. Perpendicular nonzero vectors have a zero dot product. Opposing directions give a negative one.

Worked example / A ranking surprise

Let a toy query be q=[1,2]⊤\mathbf{q}=[1,2]^\topq=[1,2]⊤. Candidate A is [2,1]⊤[2,1]^\top[2,1]⊤, and B is [4,2]⊤[4,2]^\top[4,2]⊤. These are invented numerical vectors, not measured language embeddings.

q⊤a=4,q⊤b=8\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{a}=4,\qquad \mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{b}=8q⊤a=4,q⊤b=8

In full: 1(2)+2(1)=41(2)+2(1)=41(2)+2(1)=4, while 1(4)+2(2)=81(4)+2(2)=81(4)+2(2)=8. The lengths are 1+4=5\sqrt{1+4}=\sqrt51+4​=5​, 4+1=5\sqrt{4+1}=\sqrt54+1​=5​, and 16+4=20\sqrt{16+4}=\sqrt{20}16+4​=20​.

B scores twice as highly because it is twice as long, even though A and B have identical direction. Dividing by the product of lengths gives cosine similarity:

cos⁡(q,a)=455=0.8,cos⁡(q,b)=8520=0.8\operatorname{cos}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{a})=\frac{4}{\sqrt5\sqrt5}=0.8,\qquad \operatorname{cos}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{b})=\frac{8}{\sqrt5\sqrt{20}}=0.8cos(q,a)=5​5​4​=0.8,cos(q,b)=5​20​8​=0.8

For unit-normalized vectors, the dot product equals cosine similarity. For unnormalized vectors it generally does not. Cosine is undefined if either vector is zero.

Recover an angle, or keep just one direction

To recover an angle from a cosine, use the inverse-cosine function, written arccos⁡\arccosarccos: our score 0.8 corresponds to θ=arccos⁡(0.8)≈36.87∘\theta=\arccos(0.8)\approx36.87^\circθ=arccos(0.8)≈36.87∘. Select degrees if checking this on a calculator. Cosine similarity is the score 0.8, not the angle itself.

A projection keeps the component along a chosen direction, like a shadow on an axis. Onto a nonzero vector b, the vector projection of a is:

proj⁡b(a)=a⊤bb⊤bb\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf b}(\mathbf a)=\frac{\mathbf a^\top\mathbf b}{\mathbf b^\top\mathbf b}\mathbf bprojb​(a)=b⊤ba⊤b​b

For a=[3,2]⊤\mathbf a=[3,2]^\topa=[3,2]⊤ and b=[1,0]⊤\mathbf b=[1,0]^\topb=[1,0]⊤, the multiplier is 3/1=33/1=33/1=3, so the projection is [3,0]⊤[3,0]^\top[3,0]⊤. The leftover [0,2]⊤[0,2]^\top[0,2]⊤ has zero dot product with b: it is perpendicular. This is the geometric foundation for keeping a few principal directions later. A learned layer called a "projection" need not be an orthogonal geometric projection.

3. Matrices turn representations into new ones

A matrix is a rectangular table of numbers. Its shape is rows by columns, written with a multiplication-shaped separator: 2×32\times32×3 means 2 horizontal rows and 3 vertical columns, not six coordinates in each row. Entry WijW_{ij}Wij​ is at row i, column j. In the example below, W12=2W_{12}=2W12​=2 and W23=1W_{23}=1W23​=1.

Think of a matrix as a reusable mixing rule. Each output combines the input coordinates with different coefficients. As a column-vector transformation, a matrix with 2 rows and 3 columns maps a 3-coordinate input to a 2-coordinate output.

y=Wx,W∈Rm×d,x∈Rd,y∈Rm\mathbf{y}=W\mathbf{x},\qquad W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d},\quad \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}y=Wx,W∈Rm×d,x∈Rd,y∈Rm

WWW is a weight matrix; ddd is input width; mmm is output width. Each output entry is a dot product of a row of WWW with x\mathbf{x}x.

Worked example / A tiny feature mixer

Suppose a toy support classifier has three hand-designed input features: account-related evidence, payment-related evidence, and urgency. The values and weights below are illustrative, not trained or calibrated scores.

W=[12−1011],x=[213],Wx=[2+2−30+1+3]=[14]W=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&-1\\0&1&1\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\3\end{bmatrix},\quad W\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}2+2-3\\0+1+3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\4\end{bmatrix}W=[10​21​−11​],x=​213​​,Wx=[2+2−30+1+3​]=[14​]

A bias b=[1,−1]⊤\mathbf{b}=[1,-1]^\topb=[1,−1]⊤ shifts this to [2,3]⊤[2,3]^\top[2,3]⊤. Applying an activation function would produce the layer's final output.

First output: 1(2)+2(1)+(−1)(3)=11(2)+2(1)+(-1)(3)=11(2)+2(1)+(−1)(3)=1. Second output: 0(2)+1(1)+1(3)=40(2)+1(1)+1(3)=40(2)+1(1)+1(3)=4. Then add bias entry by entry: [1+1,4−1]⊤=[2,3]⊤[1+1,4-1]^\top=[2,3]^\top[1+1,4−1]⊤=[2,3]⊤. An activation is a function applied to the result; for example, replacing each negative entry by zero leaves [2,3] unchanged but would turn [-2,3] into [0,3]. That nonlinearity matters when stacking layers.

Matrix addition and scalar multiplication are entrywise, just like vector operations: A+BA+BA+B adds matching entries and requires equal shapes; 2A2A2A doubles every entry. For A=[1203]A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&3\end{bmatrix}A=[10​23​], 2A=[2406]2A=\begin{bmatrix}2&4\\0&6\end{bmatrix}2A=[20​46​] and A+A=2AA+A=2AA+A=2A. A matrix with all zero entries is a zero matrix. These rules describe adding weight updates, not the row-by-column multiplication used to transform an input.

A strictly linear transformation preserves addition and scalar multiplication: T(au+bv)=aT(u)+bT(v)T(a\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v})=aT(\mathbf{u})+bT(\mathbf{v})T(au+bv)=aT(u)+bT(v). In particular, it maps zero to zero. A nonzero bias makes Wx+bW\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}Wx+b affine, not strictly linear. In neural-network terminology, a "linear layer" commonly includes this bias.

Another way to see multiplication: the columns of WWW tell you where unit coordinate arrows go. In 2D these are e1=[1,0]⊤\mathbf{e}_1=[1,0]^\tope1​=[1,0]⊤ and e2=[0,1]⊤\mathbf{e}_2=[0,1]^\tope2​=[0,1]⊤. They are called the standard basis: every vector has one unique expression x=x1e1+x2e2\mathbf{x}=x_1\mathbf{e}_1+x_2\mathbf{e}_2x=x1​e1​+x2​e2​. For [3,2], that is three eastward units plus two northward units. A basis is a set that reaches the entire space without redundant directions; section 6 formalizes "reaches" and "redundant."

4. Shapes are a debugging tool

One vector is a useful teaching device. An implementation processes many tokens together. We will store tokens as rows: X∈Rn×dX\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}X∈Rn×d contains nnn token representations, each with ddd coordinates. For now consider one sequence at a time.

Y=XW,(n×d)(d×m)⟶(n×m)Y=XW,\qquad(n\times d)(d\times m)\longrightarrow(n\times m)Y=XW,(n×d)(d×m)⟶(n×m)

This row-batch convention puts the weights on the right, with shape d×md\times md×m. It is the transposed equivalent of the column-vector convention above, not the same stored matrix shape. Write the convention down before reading framework code; libraries may store weights transposed.

Worked example / Row by column

X=[1234],W=[2013],XW=[461012]X=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix},\quad W=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\1&3\end{bmatrix},\quad XW=\begin{bmatrix}4&6\\10&12\end{bmatrix}X=[13​24​],W=[21​03​],XW=[410​612​]

For composition with column vectors, the rightmost matrix acts first. Rotate [1,0] by 90 degrees to [0,1], then scale horizontally by 2: the result stays [0,1]. Scale first to [2,0], then rotate: the result is [0,2]. This is why swapping matrix order changes a computation. With row vectors the written order follows the left-to-right data flow instead.

A tensor adds named array axes

In machine-learning code, a tensor usually means a numerical array with zero or more axes. A scalar has no array axis, a vector has one, and a matrix has two. An axis identifies which index varies: rows and columns are the two matrix axes. This practical array usage does not require the more abstract mathematical theory of tensors.

A batch groups several sequences for processing. Four sequences, each with 6 token slots and width 8, form an array of shape 4×6×84\times6\times84×6×8: batch, token, feature. It has 4(6)(8)=1924(6)(8)=1924(6)(8)=192 scalar entries. Apply the same 8×38\times38×3 weight matrix to every token row, and the output has shape 4×6×34\times6\times34×6×3, or 72 entries. Neither the batch count nor token count changes.

Multiple attention heads add another named axis for the different heads. In the next section we deliberately show one sequence and one head, so the arrays fit on a page. Omitting an axis in an explanation is not permission to omit it from a real implementation.

5. Read one attention head end to end

Suppose a token needs information from earlier tokens. Attention gives it a learned way to compute scores and blend information. For one head, make three projections of the same input: queries ask what information is useful, keys participate in matching, and values carry the information to be combined. 6. Span, rank, and information loss

If you can move only east or west, no number of such moves will take you north. The span of a set of vectors is every linear combination you can form from them. Adding another eastward vector gives another instruction, but no new direction.

A set is linearly independent when the only way its linear combination equals zero is for every coefficient to be zero. Otherwise the set is dependent. For example, [1,2]⊤[1,2]^\top[1,2]⊤ and [2,4]⊤[2,4]^\top[2,4]⊤ are dependent because the second is twice the first. In contrast, [1,0]⊤[1,0]^\top[1,0]⊤ and [1,0.01]⊤[1,0.01]^\top[1,0.01]⊤ are independent, despite their almost identical directions.

Formally, independence means a1v1+⋯+akvk=0a_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+a_k\mathbf v_k=\mathbf0a1​v1​+⋯+ak​vk​=0 forces every coefficient ai=0a_i=0ai​=0; k is the number of supplied vectors. The dots mean continue adding the same kind of term. Three vectors e1,e2,[1,1]⊤\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2,[1,1]^\tope1​,e2​,[1,1]⊤ in 2D are dependent even though no pair is parallel: e1+e2−[1,1]⊤=0\mathbf e_1+\mathbf e_2-[1,1]^\top=\mathbf0e1​+e2​−[1,1]⊤=0. The coefficients 1,1,-1 are not all zero. The first two already span the plane; the third adds no reachable direction.

Do not conflate these ideas

Linear dependence is an exact algebraic property. Semantic similarity is a learned, task-dependent relationship. High cosine similarity does not imply dependence. Nor do independent vectors necessarily represent unrelated meanings.

The rank of a matrix is the dimension of its column span, equivalently its row span. It counts independent directions, not how many "concepts" the model understands. For an m×dm\times dm×d matrix, rank cannot exceed min⁡(m,d)\min(m,d)min(m,d).

Here "dimension of a span" means how many vectors a nonredundant basis for it needs. The minimum symbol selects the smaller count: a 3×53\times53×5 matrix has rank at most 3. Two redundant columns can span a line (dimension 1); two independent columns in 2D span a plane (dimension 2). A zero matrix has rank 0.

Find rank by simplifying equations

Gaussian elimination subtracts multiples of rows to expose redundancy. Row swapping, multiplying a row by a nonzero scalar, and adding a multiple of another row are reversible operations: they preserve rank. Arrange leading nonzero entries progressively farther right, with zeros beneath them. This is row-echelon form; each nonzero row then contributes one independent direction.

[123246111] ⟶ [1230000−1−2] ⟶ [1230−1−2000]\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\2&4&6\\1&1&1\end{bmatrix} \ \longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\0&0&0\\0&-1&-2\end{bmatrix} \ \longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\0&-1&-2\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}​121​241​361​​ ⟶ ​100​20−1​30−2​​ ⟶ ​100​2−10​3−20​​

First subtract twice row 1 from row 2; subtract row 1 from row 3. Then swap the last two rows. There are two nonzero echelon rows, so rank is 2. You can also verify that original column 3 equals twice column 2 minus column 1. For noisy floating-point data, deciding whether an entry is effectively zero requires a tolerance; numerical rank is not a magic exact test on measured data.

7. Special directions: eigenvectors and SVD

Most vectors turn as a matrix acts on them. Some lie on lines that the transformation preserves. Those are eigenvector directions. The precise definition includes a nonzero vector:

Av=λv,v≠0A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v},\qquad \mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}Av=λv,v=0

AAA is square; v\mathbf{v}v is an eigenvector; λ\lambdaλ is its scalar eigenvalue. Positive values preserve direction, negative values reverse it, and zero collapses the vector to zero.

Worked example / Stretch along the axes

For D=diag⁡(2,3)D=\operatorname{diag}(2,3)D=diag(2,3), De1=2e1D\mathbf{e}_1=2\mathbf{e}_1De1​=2e1​ and De2=3e2D\mathbf{e}_2=3\mathbf{e}_2De2​=3e2​. The coordinate axes are eigenvector directions with eigenvalues 2 and 3. But [1,1]⊤[1,1]^\top[1,1]⊤ becomes [2,3]⊤[2,3]^\top[2,3]⊤, so it is not an eigenvector.

Eigenvalues satisfy det⁡(D−λI)=0\det(D-\lambda I)=0det(D−λI)=0. Here that becomes (2−λ)(3−λ)=0(2-\lambda)(3-\lambda)=0(2−λ)(3−λ)=0. We are looking for a nonzero vector that the shifted matrix sends to zero.

Calculate a direction, not just its stretch

Subtracting λv\lambda\mathbf vλv from both sides of the definition gives (A−λI)v=0(A-\lambda I)\mathbf v=\mathbf0(A−λI)v=0. A nonzero solution exists only when that square shifted matrix is singular, which explains the determinant equation. After finding a value of lambda, substitute it back to find v. This is not permission to choose any vector.

Worked example / A non-diagonal matrix

Let A=[2112]A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}A=[21​12​]. Calculate its shifted determinant:

det⁡(A−λI)=(2−λ)2−1=(λ−1)(λ−3)\det(A-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)^2-1=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)det(A−λI)=(2−λ)2−1=(λ−1)(λ−3)

For λ=3\lambda=3λ=3, the first row of the shifted equation is −v1+v2=0-v_1+v_2=0−v1​+v2​=0. Thus v2=v1v_2=v_1v2​=v1​; choose v=[1,1]⊤\mathbf v=[1,1]^\topv=[1,1]⊤. Not every square matrix supplies enough real eigenvector directions to form a basis. For example, a 90-degree rotation turns every nonzero real arrow away from its original line. The data-analysis construction below gives a friendlier kind of matrix, with enough mutually perpendicular real directions to describe the full input space.

Build PCA from observations

Centering subtracts each feature's mean (sum divided by observation count). Variance measures squared spread around that mean. Covariance measures whether two centered features vary together: positive products add when they rise and fall together, negative products add when they move oppositely. For n observations stored as centered rows of XcX_cXc​, the sample covariance matrix is:

C=Xc⊤Xcn−1,Cij=∑k=1n(Xc)ki(Xc)kjn−1,n>1C=\frac{X_c^\top X_c}{n-1},\qquad C_{ij}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}(X_c)_{ki}(X_c)_{kj}}{n-1},\quad n>1C=n−1Xc⊤​Xc​​,Cij​=n−1∑k=1n​(Xc​)ki​(Xc​)kj​​,n>1

Here i and j select features; k selects an observation. This is the sample convention with denominator n-1; a population calculation may instead use n. Diagonal entries are feature variances, off-diagonal entries are covariances. Swapping i and j changes no product, so C=C⊤C=C^\topC=C⊤: C is symmetric.

Worked example / A data cloud on one line

Take observations [1,2], [2,3], [3,4]. Feature means are [6/3,9/3]=[2,3][6/3,9/3]=[2,3][6/3,9/3]=[2,3]. Subtraction gives centered rows [-1,-1], [0,0], [1,1]. Their column dot products are all 2; dividing by 3−1=23-1=23−1=2 gives C=[1111]C=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}C=[11​11​].

Multiplication shows C[1,1]⊤=[2,2]⊤C[1,1]^\top=[2,2]^\topC[1,1]⊤=[2,2]⊤ and C[1,−1]⊤=[0,0]⊤C[1,-1]^\top=[0,0]^\topC[1,−1]⊤=[0,0]⊤. Eigenvalues 2 and 0 say all spread lies along the first direction. Normalize it to u=[1/2,1/2]⊤\mathbf u=[1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2]^\topu=[1/2​,1/2​]⊤. Each centered point becomes one coordinate, its dot product with u: −2,0,2-\sqrt2,0,\sqrt2−2​,0,2​. Those numbers have sample variance (2+0+2)/2=2(2+0+2)/2=2(2+0+2)/2=2; the perpendicular coordinate has variance zero.

Principal component analysis (PCA) chooses unit, mutually perpendicular directions of greatest variance. Perpendicular is also called orthogonal; unit and mutually perpendicular is orthonormal. Eigenvalues of a covariance matrix give the variance retained along each such direction, and their sum gives total variance. Centering first prevents an arbitrary coordinate origin from dominating this spread calculation.

For a toy covariance matrix diag⁡(9,1)\operatorname{diag}(9,1)diag(9,1), the first direction accounts for 9/(9+1)=90%9/(9+1)=90\%9/(9+1)=90% of total variance. Keeping just that direction reduces width from 2 to 1. It preserves 90% of variance in this example, not necessarily 90% of task-relevant information.

A short bridge to SVD

Singular value decomposition (SVD) factors even a rectangular real matrix into input directions, stretches, and output directions:

A=UΣV⊤,A:m×d,U:m×m,Σ:m×d,V:d×dA=U\Sigma V^\top,\qquad A:m\times d,\quad U:m\times m,\quad\Sigma:m\times d,\quad V:d\times dA=UΣV⊤,A:m×d,U:m×m,Σ:m×d,V:d×d

These are the full-factor shapes; smaller equivalent factor layouts are common in software. The columns of U and V are orthonormal. First V⊤V^\topV⊤ measures the input along special perpendicular directions. Then Σ\SigmaΣ, a rectangular diagonal matrix, scales them by nonnegative singular values. Finally U places the result along output directions. Unlike an eigenvector, an input singular vector need not point along the corresponding output direction, and the two spaces may have different widths.

Worked example / Keep the stronger stretch

For A=[300100]A=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}A=​300​010​​, one SVD has U=I3U=I_3U=I3​, V=I2V=I_2V=I2​, and Σ=A\Sigma=AΣ=A; subscripts on I give its row and column size. Input [2,4] first stays [2,4], then becomes [6,4,0], then stays there. The singular values are 3 and 1.

Keeping only stretch 3 gives A1=[300000]A_1=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}A1​=​300​000​​, a rank-1 approximation. The entrywise error A−A1A-A_1A−A1​ has one entry of 1 and all others zero. Its Frobenius norm, the square root of the sum of squared matrix entries, is therefore 1. Keeping stretch 1 instead would leave error norm 3. SVD's truncated largest-stretch construction minimizes this reconstruction error for a chosen rank.

That reconstruction guarantee is about matrix error, not downstream model quality. PCA can be computed through SVD of centered data; compression uses a few large stretches as a starting point, then measures task performance. A full treatment of numerical SVD algorithms is beyond the source's introductory pointer and this bridge.

03 / Similarity versus dependence

Are [1,0]⊤[1,0]^\top[1,0]⊤ and [1,0.1]⊤[1,0.1]^\top[1,0.1]⊤ dependent? What is their cosine similarity, approximately?

Reveal solution

They are independent: no scalar multiple of the first creates a nonzero second coordinate. Cosine similarity is 1/1.01≈0.99501/\sqrt{1.01}\approx0.99501/1.01​≈0.9950. Near alignment does not create exact dependence.

04 / Can a projection be reversed?

A 5×25\times25×2 matrix maps a 5-coordinate row vector to 2 coordinates. Can every input be uniquely reconstructed from the result?

Reveal solution

No. The rank is at most 2, so the mapping cannot preserve all five independent input directions. Different inputs must share outputs. Extra assumptions about a restricted input set could permit reconstruction there, but not for all of R5\mathbb{R}^{5}R5.

05 / Engineer's notebook

For a 128×64128\times64128×64 weight update, compare dense parameters with rank-4 factor parameters. Then explain why this does not prove a specific training-memory saving.

Reveal solution

Dense: 8,192. Factors: 128(4)+4(64)=768128(4)+4(64)=768128(4)+4(64)=768. The dense count is about 10.67 times larger. Actual memory also includes frozen weights, activations, gradients, optimizer states, precision choices, and other trainable components.

9. Your mental model, upgraded

Keep these connections

Represent. Transform. Combine.

  • A vector's width counts coordinates; a collection's rank counts independent directions.
  • A dot product combines length and alignment. Cosine removes length for nonzero vectors.
  • A matrix mixes coordinates; its shape tells you which inputs and outputs are possible.
  • Attention computes query-key scores and uses normalized weights to combine values.
  • Low rank constrains an update. Eigenvectors and singular vectors reveal useful structure.

You can now read the forward arithmetic of a small network. The next question is more interesting: how should its weights change when the prediction is wrong? That is where calculus enters.
